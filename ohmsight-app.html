Check the riskiest electrical items first.

Ohmsight is a research project that ranks electrical design deficiencies by how dangerous they are and how often they appear in plan check. It turns that ranking into a pre-check for designers and a review checklist for building departments, starting with solar, battery storage and emergency power.

The problem

Solar, battery and emergency power plans are often sent back for the same short list of problems: an overloaded panel busbar, no rapid shutdown method, a battery with no disconnect, a generator with no coordination study. Each correction adds a review cycle for the applicant and the building department, and some of these misses are life-safety issues, not paperwork.

Designers and installers

Pre-check before submitting

Enter the key values from the plans and see which deficiencies a reviewer is likely to flag, ranked by risk, with the calculation and the fix.

Plan reviewers

Review the riskiest items first

Get a checklist for the project type, ordered so life-safety and high-frequency items come first, then record each item and copy a correction list.

Building departments

Consistent, evidence-based review

Every item traces to an NEC 2023 section and to published sources, so any jurisdiction that adopts the NEC can use and adapt it.

How the model works

        
Every deficiency gets a severity weight and a frequency score. Their product is its risk score, out of 25, and that score orders the taxonomy, the pre-check findings and the review checklist.

        
Severity

        
Life safety (5): failure could injure or kill occupants, workers or first responders. Fire (4): failure could start or spread a fire. Equipment (2): damage or outage with limited safety impact. Administrative (1): documentation or labeling gaps that mainly cause resubmittals.

        
Frequency, from published evidence

        
Frequency is scored from public plan review checklists and industry reporting. The rules are applied in order:

        

          
5Named as a top or most-overlooked problem by a source, and cited by 2 or more sources in total

          
4Cited by at least 1 industry source and 1 jurisdiction checklist, or by 3 or more jurisdiction checklists

          
3Cited by 2 jurisdiction checklists, or by at least 1 industry source

          
2Cited by 1 jurisdiction checklist

          
1Code requirement with no published frequency signal found

        

        
A field adjustment of up to 2 points either way lets a reviewer calibrate each score with measured results from sample and pilot projects.

        
Pre-check findings

        
Each pre-check rule is tied to one deficiency in the taxonomy and takes that deficiency's risk score. Findings also show how they were found: Calculated from the values entered, Missing from package when a required document or detail is not included, or Near limit when a value is within a few percent of a code limit and should be verified.

        
Limits of this version

        
    
          
  • Frequency reflects how often an item appears in published checklists and reporting, not a measured rate from reviewed projects.
    • 
          
  • Public data is richest for residential PV. A low score can mean little published evidence rather than a rare problem.
    • 
          
  • The pre-check covers 22 automated checks for PV, battery storage and emergency power. Other items are reviewed from the checklist.
    • 
          
  • Code references are section numbers only; no code text is reproduced. References marked "Needs check" should be confirmed against the adopted edition.
    • 
          
  • No agency records were used. All data comes from public documents.
    • 
        

        
Code basis

        
NEC 2023 (NFPA 70). California Title 24 references (2025 edition, effective January 1, 2026) can be shown on the review checklist. Residential battery limits follow IRC R328 and NFPA 855 (CRC R328 in California).
